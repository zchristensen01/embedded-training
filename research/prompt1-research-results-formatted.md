### SECTION 1 — Findings narrative

**The four-stage embedded loop in your Part I is confirmed and stable.** Recruiter screen, technical screen or take-home, hiring-manager call centred on one project, then an onsite loop. This matches dated first-hand reports across SpaceX, Tesla, Rocket Lab and Anduril. Nothing in your Part I structure needs changing.

**Tesla's take-home is confirmed almost word for word.** A Blind first-hand account describes a 90-minute to 2-hour firmware take-home that is "not LC but very typical embedded questions like system design, state machine, debugging code, bit masking and manipulation" (https://www.teamblind.com/post/tesla-embedded-software-firmware-interview-process-vtm3gmrw). One candidate reported 8 questions in 2 hours; the state-machine item was a vending machine. Your "debugging existing code" note was right and you should treat it as HIGH confidence now.

**Your mutex-vs-semaphore attribution is wrong and you should fix the card.** The "roughly half of candidates claiming RTOS expertise get this wrong" figure is Niall Cooling, founder of Feabhas, writing on EmbeddedRelated: he describes the semaphore/mutex distinction as his go-to question for anyone claiming RTOS expertise and says easily 50% make the classic mistake (https://www.embeddedrelated.com/showarticle/1248.php, 2018 — older than two years, and the number is a single author's estimate, not a study). Your deck currently credits both to Barr. Correct it — if an interviewer knows the source and you misattribute it, that is a worse outcome than not citing anyone.

**The Google embedded system-design round is real, and it is the biggest hole in your system.** Blind reports describe a Google embedded loop with a dedicated embedded system-design round alongside an embedded-domain round and general coding, with one candidate describing a 25/75 split between CS fundamentals and embedded knowledge (https://www.teamblind.com/post/google-embedded-software-interview-agkmxnr7). It is not a FAANG-only artifact. Meta Reality Labs runs an in-domain design round inside a loop of roughly two phone screens, then three coding, one system design and one cross-functional round (https://www.teamblind.com/post/firmware-interview-full-loop-at-meta-reality-labs-uqmfofhd), and one first-hand account describes being rejected specifically on that design round despite passing coding and behavioural (https://www.teamblind.com/post/meta-firmware-engineer-interview-rejection-i27adtev). Medtronic has added a scaled-down design item to embedded roles. Tesla's take-home contains one as a written question.

Real prompts, sourced rather than invented: a buffered stream with incoming data and periodic outgoing data while an ISR operates on the buffer (Tesla take-home); implement your own malloc with a memory pool, implement a timer library, producer-consumer, FIFOs and multi-core synchronisation (Meta Reality Labs); a firmware logging and telemetry subsystem for an implantable device with limited flash uploading over BLE (Medtronic, via https://www.lockedinai.com/company-details/MDT).

**How it differs from a web system-design round.** Scope is one device or one subsystem, not a distributed fleet. The axes are RAM and flash budget, ISR versus main-loop partitioning, peripheral and bus topology, power states, failure and recovery behaviour, and testability. Nothing about sharding, replication, or availability tiers. Candidates repeatedly complain the round is vague and interviewer-dependent, and there is at least one report of an Apple firmware loop degenerating into a generic "design Twitter" question, so it is not uniformly well-run. Format is verbal with a shared drawing surface — whiteboard in person, a collaborative doc or drawing tool remotely. I found no report of it being a written take-home document except where it appears as one question inside a larger take-home.

### SECTION 2 — New deck cards

#### rtos

rtos ||| When would you choose a bare-metal super-loop over an RTOS, and when does an RTOS earn its keep? ||| An RTOS earns its keep when you have multiple independent activities at different rates and priorities that would tangle a super-loop, when you need blocking APIs and priority-based preemption, or when middleware like BLE, USB or TCP assumes threads. Bare-metal wins when timing is simple, RAM is tiny, and you want full determinism with no scheduler overhead. Name the cost: per-task stack RAM, context-switch latency, and a larger bug surface including priority inversion. ||| Saying an RTOS is always the professional choice, or being unable to name a single concrete cost it adds. ||| E17 ||| HIGH ||| https://www.glassdoor.com/Interview/1-Describe-the-difference-between-a-semaphore-and-a-mutex-and-when-you-might-use-each-2-when-would-you-use-an-RTOS-over-QTN_1740523.htm
rtos ||| Design a cooperative task scheduler for an MCU with no OS. What are the data structures? ||| A table of task control blocks, each holding a function pointer, a period and a next-run timestamp. The main loop does a rollover-safe unsigned comparison of (now - next_run) against zero for each task and dispatches the due ones. Tasks run to completion and never block. Discuss jitter, one long task starving the others, and how you would add priorities. ||| Using blocking delay() inside a task, or writing the due check as (now > next_run), which breaks at timer rollover. ||| E23,E17 ||| MEDIUM ||| https://www.teamblind.com/post/firmware-interview-full-loop-at-meta-reality-labs-uqmfofhd
rtos ||| What does task priority actually get you, and what does it not get you? ||| Priority decides which ready task runs when several are ready. It is ordering, not a proportional share of CPU. A high-priority task that never blocks starves everything beneath it. Priority alone does not prevent priority inversion and does not guarantee a deadline unless you have analysed the whole schedule. ||| Saying higher priority means the task runs faster or gets more CPU time. ||| E13,E17 ||| MEDIUM ||| https://www.embeddedrelated.com/showarticle/1248.php

#### sync

sync ||| A candidate tells you a mutex is just a semaphore initialised to one. What is wrong with that? ||| A mutex has ownership: only the task that took it may give it, which is what enables priority inheritance to bound priority inversion. A binary semaphore has no owner, so any task or ISR can give it, making it a signalling primitive rather than a locking one. Using a semaphore where you needed a mutex loses priority inheritance and lets the wrong context release the lock. ||| "They are basically the same, semaphores just count higher than one." Niall Cooling of Feabhas reports that easily 50% of candidates claiming RTOS expertise give exactly this answer. ||| E12 ||| HIGH ||| https://www.embeddedrelated.com/showarticle/1248.php
sync ||| Can you give a semaphore from an ISR? Can you take a mutex from an ISR? ||| You can give a semaphore from an ISR using the FromISR API with the yield-request pattern, and that is the canonical way to defer work from an ISR to a task. You must never take a mutex from an ISR: an ISR has no task identity to own it, and blocking inside an ISR is forbidden. ||| Over-correcting to "you cannot touch any RTOS primitive from an ISR", which is wrong and loses you the standard deferral pattern. ||| E12,E9 ||| MEDIUM ||| https://barrgroup.com/blog/mutexes-and-semaphores-demystified-0

#### interrupts

interrupts ||| An ISR shares a struct of a timestamp and a value with main, and the struct is volatile. Why can this still be wrong? ||| volatile forces re-reads but does not make a multi-word access atomic. Main can read a half-updated struct if the interrupt lands mid-copy, giving a new timestamp with an old value. You need a brief critical section, a double buffer or seqlock, or a lock-free single-producer single-consumer ring to get a consistent snapshot. ||| Saying volatile makes it safe. volatile is about visibility, not atomicity, and not about tearing across words. ||| E10,E16 ||| HIGH ||| https://www.teamblind.com/post/embedded-swfirmware-interviews-qet5hwmy

#### memory

memory ||| Implement your own malloc and free over a fixed memory pool for an MCU. What must you get right? ||| Maintain a free list, split blocks on allocation, and coalesce adjacent free blocks on release to fight fragmentation. Enforce worst-case alignment, return NULL on exhaustion rather than trapping, and detect double free. On an MCU you would usually argue for fixed-block pools instead, for O(1) allocation and zero fragmentation. ||| Skipping coalescing entirely, or implementing it correctly but being unable to say why firmware avoids the heap on a device that runs for a year. ||| C9,E5 ||| MEDIUM ||| https://www.teamblind.com/post/firmware-interview-full-loop-at-meta-reality-labs-uqmfofhd
memory ||| Return a block aligned to N bytes out of an unaligned pool. How? ||| Over-allocate by size plus alignment minus one plus your bookkeeping, round the pointer up to the boundary with a mask, and store the original base pointer immediately below the aligned address so free can recover it. The mask trick only works if alignment is a power of two, so assert that. ||| Rounding with modulo or division on an MCU, or forgetting to stash the base pointer so free cannot recover the real allocation. ||| C9 ||| MEDIUM ||| https://www.teamblind.com/post/system-design-interview-questions-for-embedded-software-developer-44wqfhfy

#### protocols

protocols ||| Two devices try to drive the same I2C bus at once. How does I2C arbitrate, and what do you do if two slaves share an address? ||| I2C is open-drain and arbitrates by wired-AND: a master driving a one but reading back a zero has lost arbitration and backs off, while clock synchronisation keeps the masters coherent. If two slaves have the same fixed address you cannot fix it on one bus - use an address-select pin, an I2C mux or switch, or a second bus. ||| Saying conflicts are impossible because every device has a unique address, which ignores both identical fixed-address parts and multi-master arbitration. ||| E19 ||| MEDIUM ||| https://www.teamblind.com/post/embedded-system-design-interview-questions-8vye2v1m
protocols ||| Design an interrupt-driven UART driver with ring buffers. Walk me through the RX and TX paths. ||| On receive the interrupt fires per byte or FIFO threshold, the ISR pushes into a ring buffer and returns immediately, and main drains it. On transmit main fills the buffer and enables the TX-empty interrupt, and the ISR pops until empty then disables that interrupt again. Cover overflow policy, head and tail ownership across ISR and main, and flow control. ||| Doing framing or parsing inside the ISR, or leaving the TX-empty interrupt enabled after the buffer drains, which produces a continuous interrupt storm. ||| E10,C5 ||| MEDIUM ||| https://medium.com/@akashagrawal_33749/cracking-the-firmware-embedded-systems-engineer-interview-d73a37da95bd

#### timing

timing ||| You have one hardware timer. Build an API that supports an arbitrary number of software timers. ||| Keep a sorted list or min-heap of software timers by absolute expiry, program the single hardware timer to the nearest one, and on interrupt fire everything expired, reschedule the periodics, and re-arm to the next expiry. Comparisons must be rollover-safe unsigned subtraction, and the ISR should do as little as possible. ||| Assuming one hardware timer per software timer, or polling every timer on every tick instead of arming to the nearest expiry. ||| E24,C11 ||| MEDIUM ||| https://www.teamblind.com/post/system-design-interview-questions-for-embedded-software-developer-44wqfhfy

#### state machines

state machines ||| Design the firmware for a vending machine as a state machine: coin inserted, product selected, cancel, dispense. ||| Enumerate the states - idle, collecting, sufficient funds, dispensing, refunding - and drive a transition table keyed on state and event. Track a running balance, handle cancel and refund and the exact-change case, and make dispense idempotent against a duplicated event. Keep the pure transition logic separate from the I/O drivers so it is host-testable. ||| Building it as nested if/else over globals with no explicit state enum, which corrupts the balance on cancel-mid-transaction or a double-coin event. ||| E23 ||| HIGH ||| https://www.teamblind.com/post/tesla-embedded-software-firmware-interview-process-vtm3gmrw

#### debug

debug ||| I hand you a working firmware binary and a non-working one for the same board, and the source looks identical. How do you find the difference? ||| Diff the disassembly, dump and diff every relevant peripheral register at the fault point, check the linker map for placement differences, and bisect the compiler flags and optimisation level. Verify bottom-up: power, clocks, then peripheral init order. The cause is usually init ordering or an optimiser exposing a missing volatile. ||| Rewriting code or blaming the toolchain without ever diffing register state or disassembly, and assuming identical source must mean identical behaviour. ||| H1,E1 ||| MEDIUM ||| https://dev.to/embedsys/embedded-systems-weekly-114-812
debug ||| The take-home hands you a broken codebase and says make the tests pass. How do you work on code you did not write? ||| Read the entry point and the data flow first, run it, and reproduce the failure before changing anything. Instrument at the seams, hold one hypothesis at a time, and make the smallest change that turns a test green. Look first at the classic planted bugs: missing volatile on ISR-shared data, off-by-one buffer indices, signed shifts, uninitialised memory, and reentrancy. ||| Rewriting whole functions from scratch rather than localising the defect, or changing several things at once so you cannot attribute the fix to anything. ||| H1,C1 ||| HIGH ||| https://www.teamblind.com/post/tesla-embedded-software-firmware-interview-process-vtm3gmrw

#### process

process ||| What is functional safety, and how does ASIL or DAL change how you write and test code? ||| The integrity level comes out of a hazard and risk analysis - ASIL A to D in automotive under ISO 26262, DAL A to E in avionics under DO-178C - and it dictates rigour: required structural coverage, requirement-to-code-to-test traceability, tool qualification, and independence of review. At the top level, DO-178C Level A and ISO 26262 ASIL D, MC/DC structural coverage is required. Higher level means more objective evidence, not just more tests. ||| Treating the standards as documentation overhead or "write more tests", and not knowing the level is derived from a hazard analysis and drives the type of coverage required. ||| T4,T23 ||| MEDIUM ||| https://interviewprep.org/iso-26262-interview-questions/
process ||| What does MISRA-C actually buy you, and what are its limits? ||| MISRA-C removes undefined, implementation-defined and error-prone C constructs - implicit conversions, some pointer arithmetic, dynamic allocation - so whole defect classes are prevented and the code is statically analysable. Deviations are permitted but must be documented and justified. Its limit is that it constrains constructs; it does not prove functional correctness and will not catch a logic error. ||| Claiming MISRA makes code safe or finds bugs. It prevents construct-level defects and aids analysis; it does not verify behaviour. ||| T23 ||| MEDIUM ||| https://www.tustinrecruiting.com/interview-prep/technical/embedded-systems/automotive-embedded-engineer-interview-questions-and-answers
process ||| What is secure boot, and why does it matter for a networked medical or space device? ||| Secure boot verifies a cryptographic signature over the firmware image before executing it, so only authenticated code runs, and combined with a hardware root of trust and anti-rollback it blocks both tampered and downgraded firmware. It ties directly into the field-update story: signed A/B images, integrity check before switch, rollback on failed boot. For connected medical devices it is now a premarket requirement under Section 524B of the FD&C Act, effective 29 March 2023. ||| Confusing secure boot with encryption - authenticity versus confidentiality - or claiming a CRC provides security. A CRC catches accidental corruption, not a forged image. ||| E27,E21 ||| LOW ||| https://witekio.com/embedded-software/firmware/zephyr/

#### test infra

test infra ||| Design a CI pipeline that runs tests against real hardware. What do you separate, and what gates a release? ||| Split fast host-side unit tests that run on every push from hardware-in-the-loop tests on a self-hosted runner with the device attached, and gate the release on the HIL suite. #### v&v

v&v ||| For a safety-critical device, how do you decide how much test rigour each function gets? ||| Derive it from the hazard analysis: classify each function by safety impact - IEC 62304 class A, B or C, or ASIL or DAL in other sectors - and apply proportional verification. Higher class means unit plus integration plus system testing, structural coverage, and full requirement-to-test traceability with independent review. #### inferred-trap cards (finding is sourced, phrasing and trap are mine)

memory ||| What does a bootloader actually do, and how does it hand off to the application? ||| It runs from a protected flash region at reset, decides whether to stay in update mode or boot the app, validates the application image, then relocates the vector table and jumps to the app reset handler with the stack pointer set from the app image. It owns the update transport and the integrity check; the application owns nothing about its own replacement. ||| Describing it as "the code that runs first" without the vector table relocation and stack pointer setup, which is where a hand-rolled bootloader actually breaks. ||| E31 ||| LOW ||| INFERRED

memory ||| The device hard-faults intermittently. interrupts ||| What is a hard fault, and how do you debug one? ||| It is the catch-all fault exception on Cortex-M, taken when a fault escalates or no specific handler is enabled. protocols ||| Your DMA buffer lives on a Cortex-M7 with a data cache. On an M4 with no data cache none of this exists, which is why the bug appears on a port. ||| Blaming the DMA configuration when the transfer has genuinely completed, and never mentioning cache invalidation or barriers. ||| H11,E22 ||| MEDIUM ||| INFERRED
sync ||| What is a spinlock, and would you use one on a single-core MCU? ||| A spinlock busy-waits on an atomic test-and-set rather than blocking, which is cheap when the hold time is shorter than a context switch and there is another core to make progress. On a single-core MCU it is usually wrong: if the holder is preempted, the spinner burns the entire slice waiting for a task that cannot run.
